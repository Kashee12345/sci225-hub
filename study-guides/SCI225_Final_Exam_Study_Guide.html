How this exam thinks

Nearly every question in Weeks 8–14 is one of five shapes. Learn the shapes and unfamiliar questions stop being unfamiliar.

  1. Mechanism: "Which process explains…" — they want the step, not the disease name.
  2. Discriminator: two look-alike conditions, one clue separates them (B12 vs folate → neuro signs; OA vs RA → symmetry + stiffness duration).
  3. Lab signature: a pattern of arrows identifies the disorder (↓platelets + ↑PT + ↑aPTT + ↑D-dimer = DIC).
  4. Consequence: given the lesion, predict the downstream effect (CKD → no EPO → anemia).
  5. Red flag: which finding means act now (pain out of proportion, tracheal deviation, absent cremasteric reflex).

WEEK 8

Hematologic Function

Blood as an organ: how it carries oxygen, how it stops bleeding, and the four numbers that decode almost every coagulopathy.

1 Blood, Hemoglobin & the EPO Loop ▶

Blood is ~55% plasma and ~45% formed elements (RBCs, WBCs, platelets). The hematocrit is the RBC fraction — and it is the number that rises in dehydration and polycythemia and falls in anemia and fluid overload.

Plasma proteins — why albumin matters

Albumin is the main contributor to plasma oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure — the pull that holds fluid inside the vessel. Lose albumin and fluid leaks into tissue.

Cross-week thread Low albumin shows up three more times this exam: cirrhosis (liver can't synthesize it — Week 13), nephrotic syndrome (kidney spills it — Week 11), and burns (capillary leak — Week 14). All three produce the same result: edema.

The red cell and hemoglobin

  • Biconcave disc → large surface area for gas exchange and flexibility to squeeze through capillaries. Lose the shape (sickling, spherocytosis) and cells get trapped and destroyed.
  • No nucleus, no mitochondria → maximum room for hemoglobin, and the cell can't repair itself → ~120-day lifespan.
  • Hemoglobin = 4 globin chains (2 alpha, 2 beta in HbA), each with a heme ring holding Fe2+. Each Hgb carries 4 O2.
Key idea — the oxygen content equation: almost all oxygen in blood is bound to hemoglobin, not dissolved. That is why an anemic patient can have a normal PaO2 and normal SaO2 and still be tissue-hypoxic — the saturation percentage is fine, there is simply not enough hemoglobin to saturate.

The erythropoietin (EPO) feedback loop

Tissue hypoxia → kidney peritubular cells sense it → release EPO → red marrow → ↑ erythropoiesis → ↑ RBCs → oxygen delivery restored → EPO falls.

Break the loop at any point and you get a predictable anemia or polycythemia:

2 Hemostasis & the Coagulation Labs ▶

Primary hemostasis — the platelet plug (seconds to minutes)

  1. Vasoconstriction of the injured vessel.
  2. Adhesion — platelets stick to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor.
  3. Activation — platelets change shape and release ADP and thromboxane A2.
  4. Aggregation — platelets bind one another → a soft, temporary plug.

Secondary hemostasis — the fibrin mesh (minutes)

The coagulation cascade converts prothrombin → thrombin, and thrombin converts fibrinogen → fibrin, which weaves through the platelet plug and turns it into a stable clot.

PathwayTriggerLab that measures itMemory hook
ExtrinsicTissue factor from damaged tissue (fast)PT / INRPT = Play Tennis outside (extrinsic)
IntrinsicContact with exposed collagen (slower)aPTTPTT = Play Table Tennis inside (intrinsic)
CommonBoth converge at factor XBoth PT and aPTT prolongX = the crossroads
Key idea — the four numbers. Nearly every bleeding/clotting question is solved with: platelet count (primary hemostasis), PT/INR (extrinsic + liver synthesis), aPTT (intrinsic), and fibrinogen/D-dimer (consumption + breakdown). Learn the pattern, not the disease list.
Pearl — the liver is the coagulation factory. Most clotting factors are made in the liver, and several require vitamin K. That is why liver failure (Week 13) and vitamin K deficiency both prolong the PT/INR — the liver question and the coagulation question are the same question.

3 Anemia — the MCV Framework ▶

Every anemia produces the same general symptoms because the problem is the same: not enough oxygen delivered.

Key idea — general anemia symptoms and why: fatigue and weakness (tissue hypoxia), pallor (less hemoglobin pigment + shunting away from skin), dyspnea on exertion (demand outstrips delivery), tachycardia (raise cardiac output to compensate), dizziness/headache (cerebral hypoxia), cold intolerance (peripheral shunting).

Macrocytic (>100): B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol, liver diseaseIncreased destruction (hemolysis): sickle cell, autoimmune, G6PD, mechanical

4 Sickle Cell Disease & Polycythemia ▶

Sickle cell — a one-amino-acid problem with whole-body consequences

An autosomal recessive point mutation substitutes valine for glutamic acid on the beta-globin chain, producing HbS.

Deoxygenation → HbS polymerizes → cell distorts into a rigid sickle → sickled cells occlude microvessels → ischemia + infarction + pain, and the rigid cells are also destroyed early → hemolytic anemia.

Crisis triggers — anything that promotes deoxygenation or sludging

  • Hypoxia (altitude, flying, respiratory infection)
  • Dehydration (concentrates HbS)
  • Infection / fever
  • Cold exposure (vasoconstriction)
  • Acidosis and physical or emotional stress
Red flag — autosplenectomy Repeated splenic infarction destroys the spleen in childhood. The functionally asplenic patient loses defense against encapsulated organisms (S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae, N. meningitidis) → overwhelming sepsis risk. Fever in sickle cell disease is an emergency, not a minor illness.
Key idea — crisis management follows the mechanism: hydration (dilutes and unclumps), oxygen (reverses the trigger), analgesia (the occlusion genuinely infarcts tissue), warmth, and treat infection. Notice every intervention directly reverses a trigger on the list above.

Clotting — too much clot: Virchow's triad

Virchow's triad 1. Stasis of blood flow (immobility, long flights, atrial fibrillation, heart failure) · 2. Endothelial injury (trauma, surgery, indwelling lines, smoking, inflammation) · 3. Hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, estrogen, inherited thrombophilia, dehydration). Two or more present = high DVT risk.
Cross-week thread Virchow's triad is a Week 8 concept that produces a Week 9 problem (DVT) and a Week 10 emergency (pulmonary embolism). Post-operative, immobile, dehydrated patients hit all three legs at once.

DIC — clotting and bleeding at the same time

Massive trigger (sepsis, obstetric catastrophe, major trauma, burns, cancer) → widespread microvascular clotting → platelets and clotting factors are consumed → nothing left to clot with → diffuse bleeding, while the microclots simultaneously cause organ ischemia.
Key idea — DIC is a paradox with one explanation: the bleeding is caused by the clotting. This is why the answer to "why is this patient oozing from every IV site?" is consumption of clotting factors, not a factor deficiency the patient was born with.

Pearl — TACO vs TRALI: both cause dyspnea during transfusion. TACO is a volume problem — hypertension, JVD, responds to diuresis. TRALI is a lung-injury problem — hypoxemia with normal or low BP and no volume overload.

Pediatric hematology quick hits

  • Physiologic anemia of infancy — normal nadir around 2–3 months as fetal RBCs are replaced; self-limited.
  • Hemolytic disease of the newborn — Rh-negative mother sensitized to an Rh-positive fetus; maternal antibodies cross the placenta and hemolyze fetal cells → anemia + jaundice.
  • Iron-deficiency anemia — peak in toddlers with excessive cow's milk intake.
  • Childhood ITP — often follows a viral illness; abrupt petechiae in an otherwise well child; usually self-limited.

7 Cardiac Output, Conduction & Coronary Perfusion ▶
Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume   |   Stroke Volume depends on Preload, Afterload, and Contractility
DeterminantDefinitionRaised byEffect on stroke volume
PreloadVolume/stretch in the ventricle at end of diastole — "how full"Fluid overload, venous return, sodium/water retention, regurgitationFrank-Starling: more stretch → stronger contraction — up to a point, then the overstretched ventricle fails
AfterloadResistance the ventricle must overcome to eject — "how hard to push"Hypertension, aortic stenosis, vasoconstriction, pulmonary hypertension (right side)↑ afterload → ↓ stroke volume and ↑ myocardial oxygen demand

Key idea — the Frank-Starling curve explains heart failure treatment. A normal heart pumps harder when stretched. A failing heart is already on the flat/descending part of the curve, so more volume makes it worse. That is precisely why we give diuretics (reduce preload) and vasodilators (reduce afterload) rather than fluids.

Conduction — the electrical chain of command

Pearl — atrial fibrillation ties Weeks 8 and 9 together. In A-fib the atria quiver instead of contracting: you lose the atrial kick (↓ cardiac output) and blood pools in the atrium — stasis, the first leg of Virchow's triad → mural thrombus → embolic stroke. This is why A-fib patients are anticoagulated.

Coronary perfusion — a timing problem

Key idea: the coronary arteries fill during diastole, when the myocardium is relaxed. Therefore tachycardia shortens diastole and reduces coronary filling at exactly the moment oxygen demand is highest — the core reason exertion or tachyarrhythmia precipitates angina.

Key idea — surfactant. Made by type II alveolar cells, surfactant reduces surface tension so alveoli stay open at end-expiration and require less work to reinflate. The 4-step ABG method

  1. Look at pH. <7.35 = acidosis. >7.45 = alkalosis. (7.35–7.45 with abnormal values = fully compensated — then use which side of 7.40 it sits on.)
  2. Look at PaCO2. If it moves opposite to pH, the problem is respiratory.
  3. Look at HCO3–. If it moves in the same direction as pH, the problem is metabolic.
  4. Assess compensation. Other value normal = uncompensated. Other value abnormal but pH still off = partially compensated. pH back in range with both abnormal = fully compensated.

19 The Nephron, GFR & Renal Hormones ▶

Key idea — GFR is the number that defines kidney function. Normal is roughly 90–125 mL/min. Creatinine is the better marker (produced steadily by muscle, filtered and not reabsorbed) — a rising creatinine means a falling GFR. BUN also rises but is less specific: it also increases with dehydration, high protein intake, and GI bleeding.

Autoregulation — the kidney protects its own filtration

  • Myogenic response — afferent arteriole constricts when pressure rises, dilates when it falls.
  • Tubuloglomerular feedback — the macula densa senses filtrate sodium and adjusts arteriolar tone.
  • RAAS — juxtaglomerular cells release renin when renal perfusion, sodium delivery, or blood pressure falls.
Pearl — autoregulation has a floor. It maintains GFR over a wide pressure range, but below roughly a mean arterial pressure of 60–70 mmHg it fails and GFR collapses. That is the moment prerenal AKI begins — and why sustained hypotension in shock injures kidneys first.

20 Obstruction, Stones & Urinary Tract Infection ▶

Kidney stones (urolithiasis)

  • Calcium oxalate stones are the most common. Others: uric acid (gout, high purine), struvite (associated with urea-splitting infections), cystine (genetic).
  • The universal risk factor is dehydration — concentrated urine lets crystals precipitate. Also immobility, high sodium/oxalate intake, hyperparathyroidism, family history.
  • Presentation: sudden severe colicky flank pain radiating to the groin, hematuria, nausea/vomiting, restlessness (the patient cannot find a comfortable position — unlike peritonitis, where patients lie still).
Key idea — obstruction backs up. A stone blocking outflow raises pressure behind it → hydronephrosis (dilation of the renal pelvis) → compression of nephrons → postrenal AKI. Obstruction plus infection is urgent: stagnant urine behind a blockage rapidly becomes urosepsis.

The four phases of AKI

  1. Onset/initiation — the insult occurs; hours to days.
  2. Oliguric — urine output falls (<400 mL/day). The dangerous phase: fluid overload, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, rising BUN/creatinine.
  3. Diuretic — tubules recover filtration before concentrating ability, so urine pours out. The risk flips to dehydration, hypokalemia, and hyponatremia.
  4. Recovery — gradual return of function over weeks to months; may be incomplete.
Red flag — hyperkalemia The most immediately life-threatening consequence of AKI. Potassium is normally excreted by the kidney; when excretion stops, it accumulates and destabilizes cardiac conduction → peaked T waves, widened QRS, and cardiac arrest. In the oliguric phase, potassium is what kills first.
Cross-week thread Nearly every prior week can cause AKI: heart failure and shock (Week 9, prerenal), rhabdomyolysis from crush injury or compartment syndrome (Week 14, intrarenal via myoglobin), acute hemolytic transfusion reaction and DIC (Week 8), and BPH (Week 12, postrenal). When a question gives you kidney failure, ask which upstream chapter caused it.

GERD

Incompetent or transiently relaxed lower esophageal sphincter lets gastric contents reflux into the esophagus, which has no protective mucus barrier.

  • Symptoms: heartburn (worse lying flat or after large meals), regurgitation, chronic cough, hoarseness, sore throat, dental erosion.

Key idea — skin is an organ with jobs. Barrier against microbes, fluid regulation, thermoregulation, sensation, and vitamin D synthesis. Every major skin injury is a failure of those functions — which is exactly why burns cause infection, hypovolemia, and hypothermia.

Pearl — the painless burn is the worst burn. A full-thickness burn that does not hurt has destroyed the sensory nerve endings. Students frequently mis-rank severity by pain; on the exam, less pain with a leathery white appearance = deeper injury.
Key idea — the two phases of burn danger. First 24–48 hours: fluid. 42 Ten Cross-System Threads ▶

Each thread below is a single mechanism that shows up in three or more weeks. If a question feels unfamiliar, check whether it is one of these wearing a different costume.

1. The oxygen delivery chain

W10 → W8 → W9 Oxygen must be loaded (lungs), carried (hemoglobin), and delivered (cardiac output). Break any link and tissue is hypoxic. This is why a patient with COPD and anemia and heart failure decompensates far out of proportion to any single diagnosis — and why a normal SaO2 never rules out tissue hypoxia.

2. Acid–base: lungs fast, kidneys slow

W10 ↔ W11 A metabolic acidosis (DKA, shock, CKD, diarrhea) is compensated in minutes by hyperventilation (Kussmaul). A respiratory acidosis is compensated over days by renal bicarbonate retention. Full compensation therefore implies chronicity.

3. Edema always has one of four causes

W9 · W11 · W13 · W14 ↑hydrostatic (heart failure, portal hypertension) · ↓oncotic (nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition) · ↑permeability (burns, sepsis, inflammation) · lymphatic obstruction. Every swelling question in Weeks 8–14 resolves to one of these four.

4. The kidney–blood axis

W11 → W8 Kidney makes EPO: CKD → normocytic anemia. Reverse it and chronic hypoxia (Week 10) drives EPO up → secondary polycythemia. Uremia also impairs platelet function → bleeding with a normal platelet count.

5. The liver is the coagulation factory

W13 → W8 Cirrhosis → no clotting factors (↑INR) + splenic sequestration (↓platelets) + varices (a bleeding source). Fat malabsorption → no vitamin K → ↑PT. A "liver question" is usually also a coagulation question.

6. Vitamin D → calcium → bone

W11 · W13 → W14 The kidney activates vitamin D and the gut absorbs the calcium. CKD → renal osteodystrophy. Fat malabsorption (pancreatic insufficiency, biliary obstruction, celiac) → osteomalacia. Menopause removes estrogen's brake → osteoporosis. Three routes to weak bone.

7. Virchow's triad → DVT → PE

W8 → W9 → W10 Stasis + endothelial injury + hypercoagulability creates a clot in the leg that lodges in the lung. A large PE becomes obstructive shock. This one triad spans three weeks and is a favorite exam chain.

8. RAAS: the same hormone system, three chapters

W11 ↔ W9 The kidney senses low perfusion and activates RAAS, which is lifesaving in hemorrhage and harmful in heart failure (raises preload and afterload). It also drives hypertension, which then damages the heart, kidney, brain, and vessels.

9. Portal hypertension's downstream chaos

W13 → W8 · W9 Scarred liver → portal pressure ↑ → varices (hemorrhage), ascites (edema physiology), splenomegaly → thrombocytopenia, and encephalopathy from lost detoxification.

10. Diabetes is a multi-system disease

W9 · W11 · W12 · W14 Macrovascular: CAD, PAD, stroke. Microvascular: nephropathy (a leading cause of CKD), retinopathy, neuropathy. Immune: impaired leukocyte function → infection and poor wound healing. Autonomic: silent MI, erectile dysfunction, gastroparesis. If a stem mentions diabetes, it is telling you why the primary problem is worse.

Q1. A patient with cirrhosis has an INR of 2.3 and a platelet count of 62,000. Which two mechanisms explain these findings?

A. Reduced hepatic clotting factor synthesis and splenic platelet sequestration from portal hypertension

C. Disseminated intravascular coagulation and hemolysis
D. Renal failure and uremic toxin accumulation
A is correct. The failing hepatocytes cannot synthesize factors (↑INR), while portal hypertension congests the spleen, which traps platelets (↓count). Two different consequences of the same cirrhosis.

C. Left ventricular systolic failure
D. Iron overload from repeated transfusion
A is correct. Low oxygen drives renal EPO release (polycythemia) and simultaneously constricts pulmonary vessels, raising right ventricular afterload until it fails — cor pulmonale. One stimulus, two organ consequences.

B. Folate

Which finding indicates left-sided rather than right-sided heart failure?

C. Hepatomegaly
D. Dependent peripheral edema

A. Uncompensated respiratory alkalosis
B. Metabolic alkalosis
C. Compensated metabolic acidosis
D. Respiratory acidosis
A. High pH with low CO2 (opposite directions = respiratory) and a normal bicarbonate = uncompensated respiratory alkalosis, typical of hyperventilation.

Which urinary finding localizes infection to the kidney rather than the bladder?

B. Excess progesterone suppresses the LH surge

C. Transmural inflammation
D. Fistula formation
A. UC is continuous, superficial, and rectum-upward. B, C, and D are all Crohn features — transmural depth is what permits fistulas.

Which finding is most concerning after a long-bone fracture?

Three habits that raise scores on this exam

  1. Answer the mechanism, not the disease. When a stem asks "which change explains," the correct option is a process (increased pressure, impaired mineralization, consumed factors), not a diagnosis.
  2. Find the discriminator before reading the options. Decide what single fact separates the likely candidates, then look for the option containing it. This prevents being talked into a plausible-sounding distractor.
  3. Check whether the question is asking "what is happening" or "what do I do first." Priority questions almost always want stop the harm (stop the transfusion, decompress the chest, relieve the pressure) before assessment or documentation.
