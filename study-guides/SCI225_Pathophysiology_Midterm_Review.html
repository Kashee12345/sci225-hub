How this exam thinks

The midterm asks the same five shapes the final does. Learn the shapes and unfamiliar questions stop being unfamiliar.

1 Cell Injury, Death & Adaptation ▶

Adaptation — the cell changes to survive

AdaptationWhat changesExample
AtrophyDecrease in cell sizeDisused muscle after immobilisation; brain in ageing
HypertrophyIncrease in cell SIZE (no new cells)Cardiac muscle in hypertension; skeletal muscle in training
HyperplasiaIncrease in cell NUMBEREndometrium under oestrogen; liver regeneration
MetaplasiaOne mature cell type replaced by anotherSquamous → columnar in the oesophagus with chronic reflux
DysplasiaDisordered, atypical growth — not a true adaptationCervical dysplasia; the step before carcinoma
Key idea — hypertrophy vs hyperplasia. Hypertrophy = bigger cells. Hyperplasia = more cells. Tissues that cannot divide (cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, neurons) can only hypertrophy — which is why a pressure-overloaded heart thickens rather than growing extra myocytes.
Pearl — metaplasia is adaptive but risky. The substituted cell type is better suited to the stress, but it is abnormal for that location. Sustained long enough, metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma. That sequence reappears in Week 3.

Reversible vs irreversible injury

Hypoxia → anaerobic metabolism → ATP falls and lactic acid accumulates → the Na+/K+ pump fails → sodium and water enter → cellular swelling (still reversible) → membrane and mitochondrial damage, calcium influx, enzyme release → irreversible.
Key idea — ATP is the pivot. Almost every feature of early cell injury traces back to failing ATP: the pump stops, so the cell cannot control its own volume and swells. Cellular swelling is the earliest visible change and it is still reversible.

Necrosis vs apoptosis

NECROSISAPOPTOSIS
TriggerInjury — hypoxia, toxins, infection, traumaProgrammed — physiologic or signalled
Cell fateSwells and ruptures, spilling contentsShrinks, fragments into tidy apoptotic bodies
InflammationYes — spilled contents provoke itNo — fragments are quietly phagocytosed
EnergyPassive; ATP has already failedRequires ATP — it is an active process
ScaleGroups of cellsSingle scattered cells
Mnemonic Necrosis is a Mess (swelling, rupture, inflammation). Apoptosis is Neat (shrink, package, no inflammation). If the stem mentions surrounding inflammation, it is necrosis.

Key idea — two carrier parents. Autosomal recessive: each pregnancy carries a 25% chance affected, 50% carrier, 25% unaffected non-carrier. Because carriers show nothing, the first affected child is often the family's first clue — which is exactly why the disease appears to "skip" generations.
Pearl — why X-linked recessive hits males. Males have only one X, so a single defective allele is expressed with nothing to mask it. Females need two copies, so they are usually carriers. And a father gives his son a Y, never his X — hence no father-to-son transmission.
Key idea — epigenetics. Gene expression can change without altering the DNA sequence — through methylation and histone modification, influenced by diet, stress, and toxins. It explains why identical twins diverge over time.

3 Fluids, Electrolytes & Oedema ▶

The four causes of oedema

MechanismWhy fluid leaves the vesselExample
↑ Capillary hydrostatic pressureToo much push outwardHeart failure, venous obstruction, fluid overload
↓ Plasma oncotic pressureToo little pull inward — low albuminLiver failure, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition
↑ Capillary permeabilityLeaky wallInflammation, sepsis, burns, allergy
Lymphatic obstructionNo drainagePost node dissection, tumour, filariasis
Key idea — albumin is the pull. Albumin generates most plasma oncotic pressure. Anything that lowers it — a liver that cannot synthesise it, a kidney that spills it, or malnutrition — produces oedema by the same mechanism.

The electrolytes worth knowing cold

ElectrolyteToo highToo low
Sodium (135–145)Thirst, dry mucous membranes, restlessness, seizuresConfusion, headache, seizures, cerebral oedema
Potassium (3.5–5.0)Peaked T waves, wide QRS, muscle weakness, cardiac arrestFlat T waves, U waves, weakness, dysrhythmias, ileus
Calcium (8.5–10.5)Bones, stones, groans, moans — lethargy, constipationTetany, Chvostek and Trousseau signs, laryngospasm
Pearl — potassium and pH move opposite. In acidosis, hydrogen ions enter cells and potassium is pushed out → serum potassium rises. In alkalosis the reverse happens. So a patient in DKA can have a high serum potassium while being totally depleted of body potassium.
Red flag — correcting sodium Severe hyponatremia with seizures needs treatment, but correcting sodium too quickly causes osmotic demyelination. Fast fall in sodium causes cerebral oedema; fast rise causes demyelination — the rate matters as much as the number.

4 Acid–Base & ABGs — a 4-Step Method ▶
CO2 + H2O  ↔  H2CO3  ↔  H+ + HCO3–

The 4-step method

  1. Look at pH. <7.35 acidosis, >7.45 alkalosis. In range with abnormal values = fully compensated (use which side of 7.40).
  2. Look at PaCO2. Moves opposite to pH → respiratory.
  3. Look at HCO3. Moves the same way as pH → metabolic.
  4. Compensation. Other value normal = uncompensated. Abnormal but pH still off = partial. pH normal with both abnormal = full.

Pearl — vomiting vs diarrhoea. Vomiting loses gastric acid → alkalosis. Diarrhoea loses intestinal bicarbonate → acidosis. Same system, opposite disorders, decided by which end the fluid leaves from.
Cross-unit thread Full compensation implies a chronic process, because the kidneys need hours to days. An ABG that is fully compensated is telling you about time as well as chemistry.

Two defence systems, the inflammatory response they share, and the four ways immunity turns against the host.

5 Innate vs Adaptive Defence ▶
INNATE (natural)ADAPTIVE (acquired)
SpeedImmediate — minutes to hoursSlow first time — days; fast on re-exposure
SpecificityNon-specific — same response to everythingHighly specific to one antigen
MemoryNone — identical every timeYes — the basis of vaccination

The adaptive arms

  • Humoral (B cells → plasma cells → antibodies) — handles extracellular organisms and toxins.
  • Cell-mediated (T cells) — handles intracellular threats: viruses, some bacteria, tumour cells, and transplanted tissue. CD4 helper T cells coordinate; CD8 cytotoxic T cells kill.
AntibodyRemember it as

Key idea — active vs passive immunity. Active = your own immune system responds and builds memory (infection or vaccination) — slow but lasting. Passive = you receive ready-made antibodies (placenta, breast milk, immunoglobulin injection) — immediate but temporary, with no memory.
Pearl — why the newborn is protected then vulnerable. Maternal IgG crosses the placenta and IgA arrives in breast milk — both passive. That protection fades over the first months, which is precisely when the vaccination schedule begins.

6 Inflammation & Healing ▶

The five cardinal signs and why each happens

SignMechanism
Redness (rubor)Vasodilation brings more blood to the area
Heat (calor)Same increased blood flow
Swelling (tumor)Increased capillary permeability → fluid and protein leak into tissue
Pain (dolor)Pressure from swelling plus mediators (bradykinin, prostaglandins)
Loss of functionPain and swelling together
Key idea — the whole picture comes from two vascular changes. Vasodilation (redness, heat) and increased permeability (swelling, and therefore pain). Histamine from mast cells is the first mediator; neutrophils are the first cells to arrive.

Acute vs chronic

ACUTECHRONIC
Onset/durationImmediate, daysWeeks to years
Dominant cellNeutrophilsMacrophages and lymphocytes
OutcomeResolution, or progression to chronicFibrosis, scarring, granuloma formation
Pearl — granulomas. When macrophages cannot destroy something (TB, foreign material), they wall it off into a granuloma. A granuloma is the immune system saying "I cannot kill this, so I will contain it."

Healing

  1. Haemostasis — vasoconstriction and clot.
  2. Inflammation — neutrophils then macrophages clear debris.
  3. Proliferation — fibroblasts lay collagen, angiogenesis, granulation tissue, epithelialisation.
  4. Remodelling — collagen reorganises; scar reaches only about 80% of original strength.
Key idea — what delays healing: poor perfusion, infection, diabetes (impaired leukocytes plus microvascular disease), corticosteroids (suppress inflammation and collagen synthesis), smoking, malnutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc), advanced age, and unrelieved pressure.

10 Carcinogenesis & the Genes ▶
Initiation (a carcinogen causes an irreversible DNA mutation) → Promotion (repeated exposure drives the mutated clone to proliferate — this stage is reversible) → Progression (further mutations bring invasion and metastasis).

Key idea — p53 is the most important tumour suppressor. It halts the cell cycle for repair and triggers apoptosis if damage is irreparable, which is why it is called the guardian of the genome. Lose it and damaged cells survive and divide. BRCA1/2 are DNA-repair genes; inheriting one faulty copy means only one more hit is needed.
Pearl — why cancer takes years. A single mutation is not enough. Multiple hits must accumulate in the same cell lineage — which explains the long latency after carcinogen exposure and why incidence rises steeply with age.

Carcinogens and risk

  • Chemical — tobacco smoke (the single largest preventable cause), asbestos, benzene, alcohol.
  • Physical — ionising radiation, UV light (skin cancers).
  • Biological — HPV (cervical), hepatitis B and C (hepatocellular), EBV, H. pylori (gastric).
  • Host factors — age, inherited mutations, immunosuppression, chronic inflammation.
Cross-unit thread Chronic inflammation is carcinogenic. Sustained tissue injury and repair means sustained cell division, and every division is a chance for an uncorrected mutation. This connects Week 2 to Week 3 — and explains reflux → Barrett → adenocarcinoma, and H. pylori → gastritis → gastric cancer.

14 Cerebral Perfusion & Intracranial Pressure ▶
Key idea — the Monro-Kellie doctrine. The skull is a rigid box holding three things: brain tissue, blood, and CSF. Their total volume is fixed. If one increases, another must decrease or pressure rises. Early on, CSF and venous blood are displaced and the patient looks fine — then compensation is exhausted and pressure climbs steeply.
Cerebral Perfusion Pressure = Mean Arterial Pressure − Intracranial Pressure

So a rising ICP directly reduces brain perfusion. The body's response is to raise systemic blood pressure to force blood in — which is exactly what produces the Cushing triad.

26 Eight Cross-Unit Threads ▶

1. ATP failure is the common path of cell injury

W1 → W4 Whether the cause is hypoxia, ischemia, or a toxin, the sequence is the same: ATP falls → the sodium pump fails → the cell swells → membranes rupture. It is why an ischemic stroke (Week 4) and a hypoxic cell in a test tube (Week 1) die the same way.

2. Chronic inflammation drives cancer

W2 → W3 Sustained injury means sustained repair, and every cell division is a chance for an uncorrected mutation. Reflux → Barrett → adenocarcinoma; H. pylori → gastritis → gastric cancer; hepatitis → cirrhosis → hepatocellular carcinoma.

3. Metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma

W1 → W3 The adaptation vocabulary from Week 1 is the carcinogenesis sequence from Week 3. Metaplasia is adaptive and reversible; dysplasia is disordered and premalignant; carcinoma in situ becomes invasive when it breaches the basement membrane.

4. Autoimmunity is one mechanism with many faces

W2 → W5 · W6 Loss of self-tolerance produces myasthenia gravis (ACh receptors, Week 5), multiple sclerosis (CNS myelin, Week 5), Graves and Hashimoto (thyroid, Week 6), type 1 diabetes (beta cells, Week 6), and Addison (adrenal cortex). One Week 2 concept explains five later diseases.

5. The rigid skull turns any volume into pressure

W1 → W4 Oedema is oedema — but inside a fixed box it becomes a perfusion emergency. Because CO2 is a cerebral vasodilator, the Week 1 acid–base concept directly worsens the Week 4 intracranial pressure cascade.

6. Every feedback loop breaks the same three ways

W6 throughout Gland overactive (final hormone high, stimulating hormone low) · gland failing (final low, stimulating high) · pituitary failing (both low). Thyroid, adrenal, and gonadal axes all read the same way once you know the rule.

7. Acidosis and potassium move together

W1 → W6 Acidosis pushes potassium out of cells, so DKA presents with a normal or high serum potassium despite total-body depletion. Correct the acidosis and potassium plunges. A Week 1 electrolyte principle governing a Week 6 emergency.

8. Shock is one endpoint reached by many roads

W2 · W5 · W6 Anaphylaxis (Week 2, distributive), neurogenic shock (Week 5, lost sympathetic tone), addisonian crisis (Week 6, no cortisol for the stress response), and DKA (Week 6, osmotic dehydration). All end in inadequate perfusion → anaerobic metabolism → lactic acidosis.

Six-Day Study Plan

If you have a week, use it in this order — foundations first, because everything else is built on them.

Three habits that raise scores on this exam

  1. Answer the mechanism, not the disease. When asked "which change explains," the right option is a process (ATP failure, receptor blockade, negative feedback), not a diagnosis.
  2. Find the discriminator before reading the options. Decide what single fact separates the likely candidates, then look for the option containing it.
  3. Check whether the question asks "what is happening" or "what do I do first." Priority questions almost always want stop the harm before assessment or documentation.
